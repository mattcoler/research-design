# AI policy

This is the course's working policy on AI use. It grew out of an earlier cohort's sorting of AI use scenarios and the principles they drew from it, and each year's class revisits it in Week 1 (see the [syllabus]({{ '/syllabus/' | relative_url }}#use-of-ai-in-education)). If your cohort changes it, the version in BrightSpace applies to your year.

**Foundational principle:** AI tools can accelerate research when used as scaffolding for thinking, but undermine development when used as substitutes for understanding.

## Allowed uses (with documentation)

- Asking AI to explain unfamiliar concepts, methods, or terminology
- Finding relevant papers and researchers (search assistance, but as distinct from thesis literature review)
- Grammar and clarity improvements on text you've drafted
- Brainstorming variations on research questions or methodological alternatives

You must verify understanding through independent application. You must read the actual papers, never cite AI summaries.

## Context-dependent uses (require judgment)

- Using AI-generated summaries after reading papers yourself: allowed. Instead of reading papers: not allowed.
- Having AI explain (for ex statistical) concepts you're applying: allowed. Generating analysis code you don't understand: not allowed.
- Discussing trade-offs between methodological approaches: allowed. Accepting AI recommendations without independent evaluation: not allowed.

## Disallowed uses

Some boundaries are necessary for research integrity:

- Generating drafts of texts. These demonstrate your understanding and using AI is a misrepresentation of authorship.
- Having AI analyze your research data or generate interpretations. This is the core intellectual contribution of your thesis.
- Using AI to generate peer review feedback. Peer review develops your critical evaluation skills.
- Submitting AI-generated content for assessments without clear disclosure.

## Documentation

For all AI use in assessed work, include a brief AI usage statement: what tool you used, what you asked it to do, how you verified or modified the output.

Example: "I used ChatGPT to explain Bayesian inference concepts, then worked through examples independently to verify understanding before applying to my research design."

## The defendability test

When in doubt, ask yourself:

- Did using AI help me understand better, or did it just get the task done?
- If my supervisor asked "why did you make this choice?", could I articulate my reasoning?

If the answer to any of these is "no," reconsider your AI use.

## Evolution clause

This policy will evolve. When you face AI dilemmas not covered here, apply the foundational principle (scaffolding vs. substitute), use the defendability test, and flag it for discussion. Research integrity is ongoing practice.
