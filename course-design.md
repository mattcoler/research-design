# Course design: why games?

Research methodology is a practice. Most of us can recite the difference between internal and external validity long before we can spot a validity problem in our own design. You understand selection bias once you've planned a recruitment strategy and realised halfway through that it leaves a group out. You understand reproducibility once you've tried to rebuild someone else's work from their description and failed.

So this course puts the experience first. In most classes you meet a research problem in an activity or a game, and the concepts come afterwards, in the discussion. The games are the [ethics card game]({{ '/games/' | relative_url }}#ethics-card-game) (Class 7), the [LEGO reproducibility challenge]({{ '/games/' | relative_url }}#lego-reproducibility-challenge) (Class 11) and [Research Futures]({{ '/games/' | relative_url }}#research-futures) at the poster session (Class 13). The other classes use structured activities in the same spirit: sorting AI use scenarios to build our [AI policy]({{ '/ai-policy/' | relative_url }}), clinics where groups test each other's research questions and gaps, and peer review that you give and receive throughout.

## The learning theory behind it

**Experiential learning.** Kolb (1984) describes learning as a cycle: a concrete experience, reflection on it, a concept that explains it, and a new attempt that tests the concept. A lecture starts at the concept and leaves the experience for the thesis, months later. A game runs the whole cycle in one class.

**Motivation.** Self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000) names three needs behind intrinsic motivation: autonomy, competence and relatedness. Games meet all three. You make real choices, you see quickly whether they worked, and you work them out with other people.

**Flow.** Clear goals, immediate feedback and a challenge that matches your skill are the conditions Csikszentmihalyi (1990) found for deep absorption. A well-built game creates them on purpose.

**Embodied cognition.** Physical action helps ground abstract ideas (Barsalou, 2008). Bricks that won't go together the way the instructions said make "underspecified methods" concrete in a way a definition can't.

## Debriefing does the work

A game creates an experience. The discussion afterwards turns it into something you can use in your own research, so each game ends with three questions: what happened, why it happened, and what it means for your project. Without that step a game is just a pleasant hour.

## What this isn't

The course doesn't add points, badges or leaderboards to ordinary content. The academic standard stays where it was: you still read the methods literature and you still write a full research proposal. You just read the literature after you've met the problem it addresses.

## A course that keeps changing

The course is built for small seminars and has to work for larger groups too, so its activities run in parallel groups and its feedback runs through peer review. Each cohort's experience feeds into the next version, through the course evaluation and through what we notice together in class.
