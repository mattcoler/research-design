# The FARES framework for evaluating research questions

## What is FARES?

FARES is a framework for evaluating research question quality we use in this course. A strong RQ should be:

- **F**ocused
- **A**nswerable
- **R**elevant
- **E**mpirical
- **S**ignificant

## The 5 FARES criteria

### 1/ Focused

Your research question should have clear scope with defined boundaries.

Considerations:

- Can you explain the scope in 2-3 sentences?
- Are all key terms clearly defined?
- What is specifically included and excluded?
- Is the scope manageable within thesis constraints?

**Common problems:** Questions contain multiple distinct sub-questions; scope could encompass dozens of studies; key terms are vague; no clear boundaries on population or context.

Ex transformation:

Too broad: "How can we improve speech recognition?"

Well-focused: "How does background noise affect keyword spotting accuracy for elderly users of voice assistants?"

### 2/ Answerable

Your research question should be feasible to investigate with available methods and resources.

Considerations:

- What data would you need to collect?
- Do established methods exist to address this question?
- Do you have realistic access to necessary resources?
- Can this be completed within the thesis timeline?
- Are there insurmountable practical or ethical barriers?

**Common problems:** Requires impossible-to-obtain data; would take years to complete; depends on non-existent technology; requires unavailable expertise; has unresolvable ethical barriers.

Example transformation:

Unanswerable: "What will speech technology look like in 2050?"

Answerable: "What acoustic features best predict listener-perceived naturalness in current neural text-to-speech systems?"

### 3/ Relevant

Your research question should address real needs in research or applications.

Considerations:

- Who would care about the answer and why?
- How does this connect to current debates or needs?
- What would change if you answered this question?
- Does this address a problem that users, researchers, or practitioners face?
- Would results inform future research or improve practice?

**Common problems:** Results only of interest to the researcher; problem is purely hypothetical; question thoroughly answered already; topic disconnected from field priorities; answering wouldn't advance knowledge or practice.

Example transformation:

Low relevance: "How does voice assistant usage vary between Tuesday and Wednesday?"

High relevance: "How does accent bias in voice assistants affect user trust across different cultural communities?"

### 4/ Empirical

Your RQ should be investigable through systematic observation or experimentation.

Key considerations:

- What could you observe, measure, or test?
- How would you know if you had successfully answered it?
- What would different possible answers look like?
- Can key concepts be operationalized into measurable variables?
- Is this about facts/observations or values/opinions?

**Common problems:** Question is purely philosophical or normative; cannot be tested through data; key concepts cannot be measured; relies entirely on speculation.

Example transformation:

Non-empirical: "Should AI have emotions?"

Empirical: "Do listeners perceive emotional expressions differently in synthetic versus natural speech?"

### 5/ Significant

Your RQ should lead to results that contribute meaningfully to knowledge or practice.

Key considerations:

- What contribution would answering this make to the field?
- How does this advance beyond existing knowledge?
- What new questions would your results create?
- Would the results matter even if they contradicted expectations?
- Could findings inform future research, policy, or practice?

**Common problems:** Only confirms well-established knowledge; incremental improvement too small to matter; technical change with no clear benefit; answers a question no one is asking; purely descriptive with no implications.

Example transformation:

Low significance: "What happens if we change parameter X from 0.7 to 0.8 in algorithm Y?"

High significance: "How do prosodic features contribute to perceived speaker identity in neural voice cloning, and what implications does this have for ethical voice synthesis?"

## Using FARES in practice

### Self-evaluation process

Work through each criterion systematically:

1. Write out your current RQ
2. For each FARES criterion: Rate your question (strong, needs work, weak), list specific concerns or strengths, identify what would need to change to improve
3. Prioritize which 1-2 criteria need the most attention
4. Revise: Generate alternative framings that address weaknesses
5. Re-evaluate: Does your revised question perform better on FARES?

### Peer review using FARES

When providing feedback on a colleague's RQ:

- **Clarify first:** Make sure you understand what they're asking
- **Apply each criterion:** Which does the question satisfy well? Where could it strengthen?
- **Be specific:** Don't just say "it's too broad" - explain what makes it broad and how to narrow it
- **Suggest alternatives:** Offer concrete reframings
- **Stay constructive:** Focus on improving the question, and leave the person out of it

### Common pitfalls and solutions

- **The "everything" question:** Tries to answer too many questions at once. Solution: Break into separate focused questions; choose one as primary.
- **The "someday" question:** Interesting but not feasible within constraints. Solution: Scale down to what you can accomplish, or shift to a related question with available methods.
- **The "who cares" question:** Technically answerable but lacks clear significance. Solution: Connect to broader debates, practical problems, or theoretical frameworks.
- **The "values" question:** Asks what should be, where research can only ask what is. Solution: Reframe to investigate empirical aspects of the issue.
- **The "been there" question:** Already thoroughly investigated with no new angle. Solution: Identify specific gap, new context, or novel approach that distinguishes your work.

## Relationship to other frameworks

### FINER framework (Hulley et al., 2013)

The FINER framework evaluates RQs as Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, and Relevant.

Source: Hulley, S. B., Cummings, S. R., Browner, W. S., Grady, D. G., & Newman, T. B. (2013). *Designing clinical research* (4th ed.). Lippincott Williams & Wilkins.

FARES adapts FINER by: replacing "Feasible" with "Answerable" (emphasizing methodological clarity); adding "Focused" (addressing scope definition critical for graduate work); adding "Empirical" (ensuring questions are testable through data); replacing "Novel" and "Interesting" with "Significant" (focusing on contribution over novelty per se); integrating "Ethical" considerations into "Relevant" and "Answerable" criteria.

### PICO framework

Commonly used in health sciences: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. PICO helps structure what you're asking about. FARES evaluates whether what you're asking is a good RQ.

## Examples across speech technology domains

### Automatic speech recognition

Weak: "How can we make ASR better?" - Fails: focused, answerable, significant (too vague)

Strong: "How does code-switching frequency affect recognition accuracy in Spanish-English bilingual speech, and what does this reveal about current ASR training limitations?" - Focused on specific language pair and phenomenon; answerable through corpus analysis; addresses real multilingual user challenges; testable empirically; informs training approaches for multilingual systems.

### Speech synthesis

Weak: "Can we make TTS sound natural?" - Fails: focused (what aspect?), answerable (how to measure?), significant (already well-studied)

Strong: "What prosodic features contribute most to perceived speaker identity in neural voice cloning, and how do these perceptions vary across listener demographics?" - Focused on specific features and perceptual dimensions; answerable through perceptual experiments; critical for identity preservation and ethics; empirically testable; informs both technical development and ethical guidelines.

### Affective computing

Weak: "Should AI express emotions?" - Fails: empirical (normative question), answerable (requires philosophical argument)

Strong: "Do listeners perceive emotional authenticity differently when they know speech is synthesized versus natural, and how does disclosure timing affect these perceptions?" - Focused on specific perceptual dimension; answerable through experimental manipulation; critical for transparent AI interaction; empirically testable; informs both ethics and design.

## Revising your RQ: a worked example

**Initial question:** "How does machine learning affect speech technology?"

**FARES analysis:** Focused: Far too broad; Answerable: Impossible to address comprehensively; Relevant: Generally important but unfocused; Empirical: Could be made empirical but needs specification; Significant: Too general to make specific contribution.

**Revision attempt 1:** "How does deep learning improve ASR?" - Progress: more focused, but still lacks specificity about what aspect of improvement, for whom, compared to what.

**Revision attempt 2:** "How does end-to-end deep learning compare to traditional hybrid systems for ASR accuracy with accented speech?" - Better: specific approaches, outcome, population; answerable; addresses bias concerns; empirically testable. However, significance could be stronger.

**Final revision:** "How do end-to-end deep learning systems compare to traditional hybrid approaches in ASR accuracy for non-native English speakers, and what do performance differences reveal about bias in training data?" - Clear scope; established evaluation methods; addresses critical fairness issue; quantitative comparison possible; reveals both performance gaps and underlying causes.

## Quick reference checklist

**Focused:**

- Can explain scope in 2-3 sentences
- Key terms clearly defined
- Boundaries explicit (what's in/out)
- Scope manageable for thesis

**Answerable:**

- Know what data needed
- Methods exist to investigate
- Realistic access to resources
- Feasible within timeline
- No insurmountable ethical barriers

**Relevant:**

- Clear stakeholders would care about results
- Connects to current research or practice needs
- Results would inform future work
- Addresses a real problem or gap

**Empirical:**

- Key concepts can be observed/measured
- Can be investigated through systematic data collection
- Different possible answers are distinguishable
- Tests facts/observations; values/opinions can't be tested

**Significant:**

- Advances knowledge or practice
- Makes meaningful contribution to the field
- Opens new questions or opportunities
- Results would matter regardless of direction

## Preparation for Class 2

**Before class:**

- Draft three different versions of potential RQs
- For each version, briefly self-evaluate using FARES (which criteria does it meet?)
- Identify your strongest version and bring printed copies for peer review

**During RQ clinic (Activity 1):**

- Present your strongest question and what worries you about it (1 min)
- Receive structured FARES-based feedback (5 min)
- Take notes on suggestions without defending your current version

**During question refinement lab (Activity 2):**

- Synthesize feedback received
- Systematically evaluate your question against all five FARES criteria
- Write three revised versions addressing weaknesses (scope, significance, method)
- Select your strongest revision with clear justification

**After class:** Continue refining your question based on peer feedback, your FARES self-evaluation, and connections you discover during literature review. Remember: RQs evolve. The question you have today will likely be refined further as you engage more deeply with the literature and methodology planning. That's expected and healthy.
